**Short answer:** A useful SEO audit finds problems that stop customers or search engines from reaching important pages, then ranks those problems by impact and effort. Start with crawl and indexing, review service pages and local details, test the mobile journey, and connect search data to enquiries.

![An SEO audit triage board that sorts problems by impact, effort and customer outcome](/site/blog/seo-audit-triage.svg)

## Start with the pages that matter

List the pages that support business goals: core services, locations, products, bookings and contact. Do not start with every URL on a large site. Confirm those pages load, have a clear purpose, and can be reached through links from the rest of the site.

## Audit in five passes

### 1. Can Google find the pages?

Inspect key URLs in Search Console. Check for accidental noindex rules, crawl blocks, server errors, wrong canonicals and sitemap problems. Make sure the sitemap contains preferred URLs you want considered for Search.

### 2. Does each page help a customer?

Check whether the title, heading, service details, location, price information and call to action match the searcher’s need. Look for copied city pages, stale offers and empty claims. Ask a real customer or colleague to read the page and say what they would do next.

### 3. Can people trust the business?

Check that contact details, hours, policies, licences where relevant, photos and customer evidence are accurate. Verify that forms work and do not request unnecessary information. Review public profiles and correct outdated details.

### 4. Is the page comfortable on a phone?

Test the important journey on a phone and a slower connection. Check loading, text size, sticky banners, buttons, booking steps and form errors. Use PageSpeed Insights to find technical leads, then confirm whether users experience a real issue.

### 5. Are you measuring meaningful results?

Use Search Console for impressions, clicks and queries. Use analytics or the business’s own records for calls, bookings and sales. Check whether events fire once, use the right labels and respect privacy settings.

## Prioritise without drowning in a spreadsheet

For each issue, record the affected pages, evidence, customer impact, effort, owner and next action. A good first priority is often a broken contact form or blocked service page, not a small metadata warning on a low-value URL.

| Priority | Example | First action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urgent | Contact form fails or key page is noindex | Fix and test end to end |
| High | Important service page has no clear service or CTA | Rewrite the page for the customer |
| Medium | Several pages have similar titles | Improve the pages with relevant impressions |
| Low | Minor warning on a page no customer needs | Recheck before spending time |

Do not label every tool warning as an emergency. Understand whether it affects a page people use.

## What a good report should include

A report should say what the issue is in plain words, which URLs are affected, why it matters, what to do and how to verify the fix. Avoid a 100-item list with no order. If you hire an agency, ask for evidence and a clear owner for each task.

## Turn findings into an action plan

For every finding, answer four questions: what page is affected, what evidence proves the problem, who can fix it, and how will the team know it worked? A technical issue may need a developer; a service-page gap may need the business owner; a tracking gap may need whoever controls analytics.

Put urgent customer failures at the top: broken booking flow, wrong phone number, blocked sales page or a form that loses enquiries. Next handle crawl and indexing issues affecting important pages. After that, improve content and local details. Minor warnings on low-value pages can wait.

Share a short summary with the person who will approve the work. If they cannot tell which three tasks matter most, the audit needs clearer priorities. A long spreadsheet is useful only when someone can act on it.

## Verify each fix after release

After a change goes live, test the page as a customer: open it on mobile, follow the link, submit the form or booking request, and confirm the right person receives it. For indexing changes, inspect the URL and monitor the relevant Search Console report; a recrawl or ranking change may take time and is not guaranteed. Record the release date and the metric you expect to change. If the expected improvement does not appear, check whether the fix reached every affected template and whether the original diagnosis was correct before adding more changes.

## FAQ

**How often should I run an SEO audit?**

Run a focused review when the site changes, traffic shifts or important pages are added. A full crawl can be useful periodically, but only if someone acts on the findings.

**Can a perfect audit guarantee rankings?**

No. An audit can find technical and content problems. Rankings also depend on relevance, competition and systems a site owner cannot control.

**Should I fix every warning from an SEO tool?**

No. Check the affected URL and customer impact first.
